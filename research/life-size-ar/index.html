Case study · Spatial computing

Can AR stand in for a prototype?

Physical prototypes are slow to make, and screens shrink objects and flatten their motion. I built a native iPhone and Apple Vision Pro app that places a life-size robot concept in people’s own homes, then tested whether that was enough to judge it.

Overview

Role

Designer and developer. The third stage of my MSc thesis at UNIST

Platforms

iPhone with LiDAR, and Apple Vision Pro

Stack

Swift, SwiftUI, ARKit, RealityKit, Reality Composer Pro and USDZ

Distribution

TestFlight, after passing Apple’s review on iOS and visionOS

User study

Testers aged 17 to 29, on their own iPhones, in their own homes

Expert review

Seven designers and engineers, including XR practitioners

Most products are designed long before a working prototype exists. A render on a screen can’t tell you how big something feels in your hallway, or how its motion reads from the sofa. AR can put a digital twin in the place it will live. The question is whether people can judge it well enough to make design decisions.

Two devices, two jobs.

One app, built for reach and for fidelity. The iPhone gets the study into people’s homes. Vision Pro shows what the same test feels like without a screen in the way.

iPhone · the scalable baseline

A window on the world.

LiDAR on recent iPhones finds the floor quickly and estimates scale well, and a phone reaches a much wider pool of testers than a headset. The trade-off: people view the robot through a small screen held at arm’s length, which weakens the sense of presence and tires the arm.

Apple Vision Pro · the high-fidelity benchmark

The robot in the room with you.

Stereo passthrough and a dense display make small posture changes and wheel spin legible, and eyes and hands replace controllers. The visionOS version pairs a menu window with an immersive space.

Designed to disappear into the room.

The interface follows Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines, so it feels familiar before anyone reads a word. Translucent, Liquid Glass-style panels let the room show through, which matters when the room is the point.

  1. 1

    Version one: the camera and the rules.

    Navigation, camera permission, and experiments with how buttons and overlays should sit on top of the real world.

  2. 2

    Version two: the robot and its actions.

    The full 3D model, an actions page and settings. Tapping a variant, like “Approach user, variant 1,” opens that exact animation in AR.

  3. 3

    Version three: ready for testers.

    An interactive 3D preview, and a sheet that sorts actions into base, functional and expressive, so the AR view stays clean.

Small details that sell scale.

People judge size from contact and light. The robot has to look like it’s standing on the floor, not floating in front of the camera.

Anchored to the floor.

ARKit finds horizontal planes classified as floor, and the robot’s coordinate system is bound to the ground, not to the camera.

Lit by the room.

Environment lighting estimation and a grounding shadow tie the virtual robot to the real light and surface under it.

Frozen on a pinch.

A pinch pauses any animation, so a researcher can freeze the robot at a key point in its motion and inspect it closely.

Pipeline

What people see is what engineers get.

Every animation starts as the robot’s engineering CAD, is optimized and animated in Blender, checked by my True RoboAnimator add-on, and exported as USDZ. Reality Composer Pro tunes materials before the assets ship in the app.

On Vision Pro, the model stood about half a meter tall in a living-room scene, knee height next to the furniture, matching the intended hardware.

A study that happens at home.

Testers installed the app through TestFlight and watched the robot in their own rooms. I recruited younger adults on purpose: people fluent in mobile interfaces give sharper feedback on the medium itself.

Measure 1

Which motion do people prefer?

The same actions, assembled from different motion components, rated on 10-point scales.

Measure 2

Can motion carry emotion?

Seven expressive actions shown by number only. Testers wrote down the emotion they saw.

Measure 3

Does the app get out of the way?

Ease of use, clarity of the controls, stability, and how much AR helped them read the robot.

What we found.

How testers rated the AR experience

Mean rating out of 10, on their own iPhones at home.

  • Clarity of controls8.3
  • Stability8.1
  • Ease of use8.0
  • Helped judge scale, orientation and path7.8
Scale

Size and paths read clearly. Testers could tell how big the robot was, which way it faced and where it was heading, relative to their own furniture.

Emotion

Motion alone didn’t carry feeling. “Happy” was read as excited or energetic by six testers, but “fear” was often read as efficient or guarding, and fast actions read as alertness. Many reached for pet metaphors, like “excited dog.”

Preference

Younger testers wanted predictability. The top-rated version used a static posture, a straight path and normal speed (8.0 of 10). Older adults in the earlier study had favored a more expressive, dynamic posture.

Expert check

Experts trusted the medium, with caveats.

Seven designers and engineers walked through the full framework. They rated “AR testing gives valid insights” 8.6 out of 10, the highest of any statement. They also named what AR can’t do: convey motor noise or the robot’s weight.

AR testing gives valid insights 8.6 / 10

Mean agreement from seven experts in design, robotics and XR.

What I’d tell other XR designers.

Test where it will live.

A living room answers questions about scale and paths that no render can. AR lets you run that test before the hardware exists.

Don’t ask motion to do everything.

Spatial presence came through; subtle emotion didn’t. Expression needs sound, a face and timing, not movement alone.

Match the device to the question.

Phones for reach and numbers, headsets for fine motion and presence. Build once, natively, and run both.

Limitations. The study was small and exploratory, and every tester was young. It ran on iPhone; Vision Pro was used to develop and validate the experience rather than for the study. Next, I want to move the earlier preference studies into AR and compare AR with screen-based testing directly.
