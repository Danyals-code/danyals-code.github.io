Case study · Human-centered AI

Can people still tell what’s real?

Generative video has gone from uncanny to convincing in a few years. I ran a controlled study of how well everyday viewers can spot it, and what makes them fail.

Average accuracy 60%

across 3,132 real-or-AI judgments by 87 people. Chance is 50%.

Overview

Role

Designer and researcher, first author

Team

With Prof. R. M. Karim (NUST) and Prof. KwanMyung Kim (UNIST)

Published

IASDR 2025, pictorial track, Taipei

Participants

87 adults aged 18 to 70, none with professional AI, graphics or video experience

Design

Within-subjects online study, binary forced choice, randomized order

Analysis

Logistic regression with Holm–Bonferroni correction

Most research on synthetic media focuses on deepfakes of faces and voices. But designers and media artists now use AI video in three quite different ways, writing clips from text, animating still images, and extending real footage beyond its frame. Nobody had compared them side by side.

Question 1

How accurately can untrained viewers detect each generation technique?

Question 2

How does the content’s theme change detection across techniques?

A balanced set of 36 clips.

Half real, half AI, spread evenly across three themes. Pilot tests showed that AI handles some subjects, like static objects, far better than people or motion, so varied themes kept the comparison fair.

Making the AI clips

One pipeline, so only the technique varied.

Every AI clip was produced in ComfyUI, whose node graphs let us replicate diffusion steps, guidance and prompt weights across methods. Five designers generated candidates for each condition. We kept the best one by visual fidelity, temporal coherence and thematic relevance.

Real clips came from publicly available stock and archival footage matched to the same themes. All 36 were trimmed to 8–12 seconds, standardized to 1080p and shown without sound.

Procedure

One question, 36 times.

  1. Each clip plays alone, in random order, as many times as the participant likes.

  2. “Do you believe this video is real or AI-generated?” Real, or AI.

  3. A short break after 18 clips to limit fatigue.

  4. Participants weren’t told how many clips were AI, only to judge each one on what they saw.

What people got right, and wrong.

Accuracy by source

Share of clips classified correctly. For real footage, correct means “real.”

  • Image-to-video79%
  • Real footage64%
  • Outpainting49%
  • Text-to-video43%

Text-to-video fooled people more often than chance, and outpainting sat right at it. Image-to-video left motion cues that viewers could pick up on.

48%

Nature looked fake. Real environmental footage was judged correctly only about 48% of the time. Pristine natural scenery often read as synthetic.

61% → 32%

Context flips the result. Outpainting was easy to catch in environment scenes (61%) but hard in historical ones (32%).

75–83%

Some tells survive every theme. Image-to-video was spotted consistently in film, history and environment clips.

What it means.

Knowing AI exists makes real footage suspect.

Real clips were judged correctly only 64% of the time. Once viewers know AI may be present, even authentic images start to look synthetic.

Realism depends on context, not just the model.

Realism isn’t a fixed property of a model. It emerges from what a clip shows. Creators can use that deliberately to set how real a piece feels.

Teach context, not just artifacts.

Media-literacy efforts and detection tools should cover contextual cues as well as model-specific artifacts, because intuition alone isn’t enough.

Limitations and next steps. Short, silent clips viewed online limit ecological validity. Future studies should add techniques such as inpainting, ControlNet and depth-aware video, collect confidence ratings, broaden the demographics, and test whether training improves detection over time.
