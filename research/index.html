Research

Human-centered research in spatial and intelligent systems

My research examines how people experience spatial and intelligent systems, combining mixed-reality and AR prototypes, controlled perception studies and participatory workshops. It was conducted primarily at UNIST’s Integration and Innovation Design Lab (IID Lab) under Prof. KwanMyung Kim.

Publications

Four peer-reviewed papers and an MSc thesis. A paper based on the thesis is in review at ACM CHI 2027.

  • 2026 · Conference paper · KSDS 2026

    Assessment of Multi-View Consistency in Automated Product Form Analysis

    Danyal Sarfraz, KwanMyung Kim

    Can a small set of product images replace CAD for measuring form? For 20 of 23 form parameters, sixteen views matched a 180-view baseline at 90% consistency or better.

    Case study

    1. Method From images to 23 form parameters

      Each image becomes a silhouette mask and a monocular depth map (Depth-Anything-V2-Small). Twenty-three established form parameters are computed from them: 15 from the mask alone and 8 with depth as well. The parameters are not new; the contribution is extracting all 23 automatically from a small set of images. Python with OpenCV and scikit-image.

    2. Experiment 16 views against 180

      Seven products (Lemmy, a Sony PSP-1000, a Teenage Engineering TP-7, a game controller, a Nokia handset, a Marshall speaker and a Canon lens), each rendered on a full 360° turntable at 16 views (every 22.5°) and 180 views (every 2°). Consistency = 1 − |v₁₆ − v₁₈₀| / |v₁₈₀|, averaged across products.

      20 of 23 parameters were highly consistent (90% or more), led by solidity (99.9%), centroid balance, rectangularity and primitive fit (99.8%). Sub-band entropy (83.4%) and axis count (80.4%) were moderate, and concavity part count (76.3%) fell lower because it depends on a threshold.

      Limits: seven products, rotated about one axis. The study shows stability across view counts, not geometric accuracy; linking the parameters to expert ratings is the next test.

  • 2026 · MSc thesis · UNIST

    A Framework for Designing Contextually Appropriate Physical Actions in Non-Humanoid Robots

    Danyal Sarfraz · advised by Prof. KwanMyung Kim

    A three-stage framework for a robot’s physical actions: co-design them with users, make them physically feasible with a Blender add-on, then test them at life size in AR before the hardware exists.

    Case study

    UNIST, Department of Design. Committee: Prof. James Self and Prof. Jaeyeon Lee. Defended on December 16, 2025.

    1. Stage 1 Designing actions with users

      The component method from the IASDR 2025 paper, plus an early round with 36 younger, multinational participants.

      Younger participants favored curved paths, while older adults preferred straight ones, so motion preferences vary by user group. Actions built from older adults’ preferred components scored 7.7 out of 10, against 5.3.

    2. Stage 2 True RoboAnimator, tested with Blender users

      A Blender add-on that flags frames where a two-wheeled robot would have to slide sideways, repairs infeasible paths, computes wheel angles and RPM within limits, and exports data for engineers and AR. External Blender users tried it in think-aloud sessions.

      Stability 8.6 and workflow fit 8.4 out of 10, with no crashes reported. Ease of understanding scored lowest (6.8), so the next version should show its intermediate steps. It later passed Blender’s official review and has more than 7,000 downloads.

    3. Stage 3 Life-size AR at home

      A native iPhone and Apple Vision Pro app anchors the robot to the floor at true scale. Participants aged 17 to 29 used it on their own iPhones at home.

      Clarity 8.3, stability 8.1 and ease of use 8.0 out of 10; 7.8 for judging scale, orientation and path. Motion alone did not convey emotion: “fear” read as efficient or guarding, and many participants described Lemmy as pet-like.

    4. Review Seven experts on the whole framework

      Four experts from design and three from engineering walked through the framework and rated their agreement out of 10.

      • AR testing gives valid insights8.6
      • Clear and easy to follow8.4
      • Bridges designers and engineers8.0
      • Adapts to other robots and contexts7.6

      Their concerns set the next steps: screen, sound and distance cues are not yet covered, AR cannot convey motor noise or weight, and the validator’s one-click behavior is hard to trust.

  • 2025 · Full paper · IASDR 2025, Taipei

    A Component-Based Approach for Developing Actions in Non-Humanoid Elderly Care Robots

    Danyal Sarfraz, Ulugbek Vahobjon Ugli Ismatullaev, KwanMyung Kim

    Robot motion, broken into four components that older adults can judge: speed, path, posture and turn type. Actions assembled from their preferred components scored highest, 7.7 out of 10 against 5.3 for the opposite choices.

    Case study

    Builds on the action catalogue from the KSDS 2025 paper. All stimuli used a digital twin of Lemmy, animated in Blender within the robot’s physical limits, so motion could be studied before the hardware existed.

    1. Study 1 Expert interviews

      Interviews with professional designers about how they reason about motion: the variables they adjust and how they break an action down. Thematic analysis.

      Four components that are practical to design with and easy for older adults to perceive: speed, path, posture and turn type. Rhythm, repetition and acceleration were set aside to keep the tests simple.

    2. Study 2 Paired comparisons with 25 older adults

      Adults aged 65 and over, in Pakistan, watched pairs of animations that differed in one component and chose the one they preferred, in randomized order.

      • Normal speedvs fast80%
      • Straight pathvs curved68%
      • Dynamic posturevs static64%
      • Smooth arc turnvs sharp pivot64%

      Path, posture and turn-type preferences did not reach statistical significance in this sample and are treated as directional.

    3. Study 3 Validation with 15 new older adults

      Three actions (approach the user, return to dock and a dance), each in four versions: built from preferred components, from non-preferred ones, and two random mixes. Each of the 12 videos rated out of 10.

      • Preferred components7.7
      • Random mix A6.3
      • Random mix B6.2
      • Non-preferred5.3

      Preferred components produced the highest-rated actions, and “return to dock” built from them scored 8.5. For the dance, a random mix came close (6.7 against 7.0): expressive actions tolerate more variation than task actions.

  • 2025 · Pictorial · IASDR 2025, Taipei

    Synthetic Realities: Evaluating Human Ability to Distinguish AI-Generated Videos from Real Footage

    Danyal Sarfraz, Raja Mubashar Karim, KwanMyung Kim

    87 people judged 36 clips as real or AI-generated and were right 60% of the time, against 50% for guessing. Text-to-video fooled them most, and real footage was often taken for AI.

    Case study

    1. Design A controlled online study

      Within subjects, binary forced choice (“real or AI?”), randomized order, with a short break halfway. 87 adults aged 18 to 70, none with professional AI, graphics or video experience, were not told how many clips were AI.

    2. Stimuli 36 clips, three techniques, three themes

      18 real clips from stock and archival footage, and 18 made in ComfyUI with text-to-video (Google Veo 3), image-to-video (LTX Video) and outpainting (Wan VACE), across film, history and environment themes. Five designers generated candidates for each condition; all clips were trimmed to 8–12 seconds, 1080p and silent.

    3. Results Accuracy depended on technique and theme

      • Image-to-video79%
      • Real footage64%
      • Outpainting49%
      • Text-to-video43%

      Text-to-video fell below chance and outpainting sat at chance, while image-to-video kept motion cues that gave it away. Real environmental clips were classified correctly only about 48% of the time, and outpainting was detected in 61% of environment trials but only 32% of historical ones.

      Logistic regression with Holm–Bonferroni correction: technique, theme and their interaction were all significant (p < .001), across 3,132 observations. Limits: short, silent clips viewed online.

  • 2025 · Conference paper · KSDS 2025, Seoul

    Developing a Hierarchically Structured Action Catalogue for an Elderly Care Robot through Participatory Design

    Danyal Sarfraz, Ulugbek Ismatullaev, KwanMyung Kim

    A participatory workshop with six industrial-design researchers produced more than 40 action ideas for a care robot, organized into a hierarchical catalogue. All three teams favored social and emotional presence over pure efficiency.

    Case study

    1. Workshop Actions from everyday scenarios

      Six industrial-design researchers, working in pairs, built user stories around everyday moments, such as an older adult preparing breakfast or searching for a misplaced item, and listed each point where Lemmy could help.

    2. Results A 22-action catalogue

      More than 40 ideas became 22 actions, 14 functional and 8 expressive, organized into a hierarchy by how often teams proposed them. Five appeared in every team: approach the user, follow, return to dock, reposition and nod.

      Each team clustered its ideas differently: by type of task, by context and by interaction with the user. All three favored social and emotional presence over pure efficiency, such as pausing near the user until acknowledged.

    An intelligent, customized care product-service system for older adults

    My part was the motion of Lemmy, the project’s two-wheeled companion robot: from designing its actions with older adults to testing them at life size in AR.

    • Action design with older adults. A designer workshop, expert interviews and preference studies with 40 older adults, published at KSDS 2025 and IASDR 2025.

Personal projects

Work outside the funded projects.

  • 2024 · Mixed reality · First place, 3rd Korea Heritage Digital Contents Contest

    Bangudae XR: the petroglyphs, indoors

    Visitors see the Bangudae petroglyphs only from across a stream. I captured the site and built a Meta Quest 3 app that brings its heritage into any room.

    Case study

    1. Fieldwork The site and how people see it

      Field documentation along Ulsan’s Daegokcheon Stream: the landscape, the rock surfaces, and how visitors view the carvings today, from across the water and often through a viewing scope.

    2. Capture Photogrammetry and Gaussian splatting

      Photogrammetry turned rock surfaces into textured meshes; AI-based Gaussian splatting (Luma AI) captured buildings and larger structures; 360° panoramas covered the landscape.

    3. App Passthrough mixed reality on Meta Quest 3

      Built in Unreal Engine 5. Five scanned artifacts in chronological order, from Mesozoic dinosaur footprints to the Joseon Dynasty, with only Previous, Home and Next to keep it easy to learn and test, plus panoramas and a photo gallery.

      First place at the 3rd Korea Heritage Digital Contents Contest (2024), organized by the Korea Heritage Service and the Korea National University of Heritage, after a written preliminary round and an on-site final presentation.

  • 2025 · Interaction experiments

    Pointing, window snapping and fish-tank VR

    Three focused studies of established HCI problems: Fitts’ law across three input devices, physics-based window snapping, and head-coupled perspective from a webcam.

    The first two began in UNIST’s Advanced Human–Computer Interaction course in 2025.

    1. Pointing Fitts’ law, three ways

      A mouse, a Wacom Cintiq Pro stylus and an iPad operated with a mouse, on WebFitts tasks at three amplitudes (200, 400 and 800 px) and three widths (40, 80 and 160 px), 11 targets per condition, one session per device.

      Throughput in bits per second. Higher is better.

      • Mouse5.6
      • Wacom Cintiq Prostylus4.0
      • iPad with mouse2.2

      The mouse was fastest and most accurate. The stylus was held back by misclicks and calibration offsets, and the iPad had the lowest throughput, with limited pointer control within apps. Both missed about one target in ten.

    2. Technique Physics-based window snapping

      A window-management prototype in Unreal Engine Blueprints. Windows move with velocity and a speed limit, check for collisions, and snap to screen edges and corners, with tunable snap strength and distances.

    3. Perspective Fish-tank VR with webcam head tracking

      In the browser, webcam face tracking drives an off-axis projection, so the monitor behaves as a window onto a 3D scene that responds to head movement. Builds on work by Ware, Arthur and Booth, and by Deering. All video is processed on-device.
