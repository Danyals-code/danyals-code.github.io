Case study · Human–robot interaction

How should a care robot move?

Lemmy is a two-wheeled companion robot for older adults. It can’t gesture like we do, so how it moves carries much of what it communicates. My research asked how that motion should be designed, and who should decide.

Overview

Role

Designer and researcher, first author

Team

IID Lab, UNIST, with U. V. U. Ismatullaev and Prof. KwanMyung Kim

Timeline

2024 – 2025

Methods

Designer workshop, expert interviews, paired-comparison study, validation study, thematic analysis

Participants

6 designers in the workshop, 25 adults aged 65+ for preferences, 15 more for validation

Outputs

KSDS 2025, IASDR 2025, and a CES 2025 Innovation Award with the Lemmy team

Co-designing with older adults matters, but long sessions are tiring and hard to repeat. Robot motion usually gets settled late, by intuition, once hardware exists. So I asked a different question: what if we learned people’s preferences once, for a few simple motion “ingredients,” and built every new action from them?

Three gaps in how robot motion gets designed.

  1. 1

    Theory rarely becomes method.

    There are good frameworks for describing expressive motion, but few step-by-step processes a designer can actually follow.

  2. 2

    Co-design wears people out.

    Cognitive load, fatigue and unfamiliar technology make intensive co-creation sessions with older adults hard to sustain.

  3. 3

    No reusable building blocks.

    There was no validated way to break an action into parts that can be tuned once and recombined into new actions.

Four steps, from ideas to evidence.

Everything was tested on a high-fidelity digital twin: Lemmy’s CAD model, brought into Blender and animated only within the robot’s real limits. That let us iterate quickly, control every variable and run studies while the hardware was still being built.

Step 1 · Action catalogue

What should a companion robot do all day?

I ran a participatory workshop with six designers in three teams. Working from everyday stories, such as an older adult preparing breakfast or looking for a misplaced item, they listed every moment where Lemmy could help, then clustered the ideas.

The teams produced more than 40 action ideas, which I consolidated into a catalogue of 22 actions: 14 functional and 8 expressive. Five came up in every team: approach the user, follow, return to dock, reposition and nod.

Step 2 · Components

Find the knobs designers actually turn.

Interviews with professional designers surfaced how they think about motion: the variables they adjust and how they break an action down. Four components were both easy to design with and easy for older adults to notice.

  • Speed
  • Path
  • Posture
  • Turn type

Thematic analysis also surfaced rhythm, repetition and acceleration, which I set aside to keep the tests simple.

Step 3 · Preferences

Ask older adults, one component at a time.

Twenty-five adults aged 65 and over, all in Pakistan, watched pairs of 3D animations that differed in a single component and picked the one they preferred. Trial order was randomized.

  • Speed: normal (about 0.5–0.7 m/s, a small pet’s walking pace) or fast (over 0.7 m/s)
  • Path: straight or gently curved
  • Posture: upright and static, or leaning with the action
  • Turn type: a sharp pivot or a smooth arc

Step 4 · Validation

Do the preferences add up to better actions?

I built three actions, approach the user, return to dock and a dance, in four variants each:

  • V1 from the preferred components
  • V2 from the non-preferred ones
  • V3 and V4 from random mixes

A new group of 15 older adults, who had never seen the component tests, rated each of the 12 videos out of 10 and explained their ratings.

What we found.

Component preferences of older adults

Share of 25 participants choosing each variant

  • Normal speedvs fast80%
  • Straight pathvs curved68%
  • Dynamic posturevs static64%
  • Smooth arc turnvs sharp pivot64%

Posture, path and turn-type preferences did not reach statistical significance in this sample, so treat them as directional. The result was a default profile for Lemmy: moderate pace, straight paths, smooth turns and a body that leans into what it’s doing.

Expressive actions tolerate more. For the dance, a random mix came close to the preferred version, and opinions were more divided.

What designers can take from this.

Learn preferences once. Reuse them everywhere.

A small library of preferred component values acts as a design prior. New actions can be composed from it, and only deliberate departures need another test. That saves participants’ time, which is precious in eldercare.

Predictable for tasks, freer for expression.

Straight paths and smooth turns matter most for task actions, where people need to read the robot’s intent. Expressive actions like dancing can relax those rules without hurting acceptance.

Test before the hardware exists.

Animation bounded by the real robot’s limits made it possible to study motion while the physical prototype was still in development, without tying design decisions to what the hardware could do that week.

Limitations. Each component had only two variants, to keep the tests easy, so fine distinctions weren’t captured. Animation can’t reproduce live timing, personal space or real reactions. The samples were small and from a single country, and the next step is embodied testing. Two of these limits shaped what I built next: a tool that keeps animations physically possible, and AR tests that put the robot at life size in real rooms.
