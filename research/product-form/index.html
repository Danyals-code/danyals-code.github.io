Case study · Computational design · KSDS 2026

Measuring product form from a handful of images.

Data-driven design needs numbers that describe a product’s shape. I tested whether sixteen ordinary renders can replace 3D CAD, which is proprietary and rarely available, for that job.

One of the seven products analyzed. Each was rendered on a full 360° turntable.

Project facts

Authors

D. Sarfraz and K. Kim, UNIST

Venue

KSDS 2026 International Conference

Stack

Python with OpenCV and scikit-image, and Depth-Anything-V2-Small via Hugging Face Transformers

A short vector describing elongation, symmetry, outline irregularity or apparent part count helps compare competitors, track how a design evolves, or link form to judgments like perceived quality. CAD would be the ideal input, but it’s rarely available. Product images are everywhere, and they’re what human experts judge anyway.

Why

Two signals, 23 numbers.

Each image is split into a silhouette and a depth map, then summarized with 23 established form parameters. The individual parameters aren’t new. The contribution is getting all 23 automatically from a few images.

  1. 1

    Silhouette mask

    From the alpha channel, or luminance thresholding plus a largest-contour fill.

  2. 2

    Monocular depth

    Depth-Anything-V2-Small predicts depth from a single RGB image, normalized within the mask.

  3. 3

    23 parameters

    Symmetry, proportion, curvature, visual complexity, convexity, balance and primitive fit. Fifteen use the mask only; eight blend in depth.

The test. Seven products were each rendered on a full 360° turntable at two set sizes, 16 views (every 22.5°) and 180 views (every 2°): Lemmy, a Sony PSP-1000, a Teenage Engineering TP-7, a game controller, a Nokia handset, a Marshall speaker and a Canon lens. For each parameter, consistency = 1 − |v₁₆ − v₁₈₀| / |v₁₈₀|, averaged across products.

Sixteen views were enough.

20 of 23

Highly consistent (90% or more). Led by solidity (99.9%), centroid balance, rectangularity and primitive fit (99.8%), convexity index (99.7%) and symmetry (99.6%).

2

Moderate (80–90%). Sub-band entropy (83.4%) and axis count (80.4%), sensitive because of tiny native scales or integer thresholds.

1

Lower (under 80%). Concavity part count (76.3%) is threshold-sensitive and drops on products with almost no concavities.

Why it matters: throughput.

A 23-parameter score takes seconds from sixteen renders, which product catalogs often already have. At the scale of hundreds or thousands of products, that makes it possible to pair form parameters with expert evaluations and build the datasets that learning models need.

What it doesn’t show yet.

Seven products, rotated around one axis only, with no pitch variation. The integer-count parameters need rescaling before they can be reported alongside the others. The study shows stability across view counts, not geometric accuracy. Linking the vectors to expert ratings is the next test.
